Robots in low-income smallholder agriculture: Africa first

Farm robots are getting cheap. In a region where labor is abundant and food is imported, will they create jobs or destroy them? This page explains the answer step by step, and ends with a plan to prepare.

In one minute

Target the bottleneck, not the robot

Most thinking about robots comes from rich countries, where workers are scarce and expensive. African farming is the opposite case: farms are small, families do most of the work, and wages are low. So the useful question is not “do robots raise productivity?” It is “when do they raise it without destroying the jobs rural people depend on?”

  • Robots create jobs when they remove a peak-season bottleneck. If a farm cannot find workers at planting or harvest, land stays idle or work is done late. A robot that does the peak task lets the farm grow more, and that creates work for people in every other task.
  • Robots destroy jobs when they only replace workers who are available and cheap. Nothing more is produced, and the workers lose their income.
  • Food imports tilt the balance toward jobs. While a country imports a crop, extra local output replaces imports and prices hold. Farms can then expand without hurting each other.
  • This window will not stay open. Once a country feeds itself, more robots release labor from farming. Policy should use the window now and prepare for what follows.

Step 1

What is a “robot” on a small farm?

A tractor needs a driver for every minute it works. A robot is different: it is a machine that carries out a task with little or no human control. On small farms this means things like a drone that sprays or sows a field by itself, a small autonomous weeder, or a self-guided planter or harvester.

Two features matter for everything that follows.

Robots arrive as a service

Few smallholders can buy a robot. They pay a provider per hectare, the same way many already hire a tractor or a thresher. So the price that matters is the service fee, not the price of the machine.

Robots do one task, not the whole crop

A spraying drone does not weed, harvest or carry sacks. Each hectare still needs people for the other tasks. This is why one robot can raise the demand for labor.

This is no longer a distant prospect. Drone services already cover 30% or more of cropland in China, Japan, South Korea and Thailand, and hiring one usually costs about the same as hiring workers, or less (Belton et al., 2025). A widely used spraying drone sells for about US$5,000 in China. As prices fall, the technology will reach African farms whether or not governments have planned for it.

Step 2

Why Africa is a different case

Four facts set the scene. Together they explain why results from rich countries cannot simply be copied.

  • Food demand is outrunning supply. Cities are set to double in size. African farm output has grown mainly by using more land and more labor, not by producing more per worker or per hectare. Imports fill the gap. They cost foreign exchange and expose countries to world price spikes and supply disruptions.
  • Farming is still done by hand. This keeps output per worker low. It also means there is a lot of work that machines could, in principle, take over.
  • Jobs are the central worry. Millions of young people look for work each year and few find formal jobs. A technology that removes farm work without creating other work would make this worse.
  • Farms are small and scattered. A machine must travel between many small plots, which raises its cost per hectare.

Step 3

The labor puzzle: too many workers, and too few

Rural Africa has plenty of labor across the year. Yet farmers often say they cannot find workers. Both statements are true, and the picture at the top of this page shows why.

Farm work is not spread evenly. Planting must be done in the few days after the rains start. Harvest must be done before the crop spoils. Neighbours face the same dates, so everyone needs workers at the same time. For a few weeks, labor is short. For the rest of the year, there is little to do.

The cost of missing the window is high. Delayed sowing or weeding can cut yields by up to 1% for each day of delay (Malabo Montpellier Panel, 2018). And as young people move to towns, the peaks get tighter.

The key idea. A useful analysis must hold both facts at once: underemployment on average, and a real labor shortage at the peak. Whether a robot helps or harms depends on which of the two it meets.

Step 4

The farmer’s choice: is the robot worth its price?

Take one hectare. It needs two kinds of work:

  • The peak task (land preparation, planting or harvest), which must be done inside a short window.
  • Other tasks (weeding, post-harvest handling, marketing), done when labor is easy to find.

A robot service offers to do the peak task for a fee. The farmer compares that fee with the value of the human labor it replaces. The catch is that this value is not always the market wage.

If workers can be hired at the peak

An hour of peak labor is worth the market wage. The robot pays only if its fee is below the wage bill it replaces.

If workers cannot be found

Without the peak task, the hectare produces nothing. So an hour of peak labor is worth the whole net income of the hectare, divided by the hours needed. Economists call this the shadow wage. It can be several times the market wage.

Example. A hectare yields a crop worth 400. Other tasks cost 300 hours × 0.5 = 150. The peak task needs 150 hours. If no workers can be found at the peak, each peak hour is worth (400 − 150) / 150 ≈ 1.7, more than three times the market wage of 0.5. So the robot service pays at any fee below 250 per hectare, although hired workers would cost only 75 if they were available.

Step 5

Jobs: created or destroyed?

Now count the hours. When a robot serves one more hectare, labor demand changes in three ways.

+ Expansion

If the robot makes new production possible, people are needed for all the other tasks on that hectare.

− Displacement

If the robot does work that people were already doing, those hours are lost.

+ New service tasks

Someone must operate, move, charge and repair the robot.

Everything turns on one number: the share of robot work that expands production, instead of replacing people on work they already did. Call it ψ (psi). It runs from 0, where robots only replace workers, to 1, where every robot-hectare is new production.

Change in work per robot-hectare = ψ × b − (1 − ψ) × a + e a = peak-task hours per hectare · b = hours of other tasks per hectare · e = service hours each robot-hectare creates
Robots create jobs when ψ is above (a − e) / (a + b)

Example. With 150 peak hours, 300 other hours and 10 service hours per hectare, the threshold is 140 / 450 ≈ 0.31. So robots create jobs if at least about a third of their work expands production.

The threshold is lower, and job creation more likely, when the rest of the crop cycle needs a lot of labor (rice, vegetables, cotton) and when the robot brings many service jobs with it.

Step 6

Can a robot service business survive?

A robot that would help farmers is useless if no one can make a living providing it. A service provider has a fixed cost each season: the machine, finance, insurance, staff. It earns a margin on every hectare served. To break even it needs enough hectares.

Hectares needed to break even = fixed cost ÷ margin per hectare The provider survives only if this is below what it can serve: the smaller of its capacity (hectares per day × days in the window) and local demand.

Three features of African farming make this hard:

  • Short windows. If planting lasts three weeks, the robot earns for three weeks and sits idle the rest of the year.
  • Scattered plots. Travel between small fields eats into the margin.
  • Thin demand. Too few paying farmers within reach.

The remedies follow directly: robots that do several tasks across the year, providers that move between zones as seasons shift, farms grouped through cooperatives or aggregators so that plots are close together, and finance that spreads the fixed cost over several years.

Step 7

The whole economy: imports change the answer

So far we looked at one farm. For a country, the question is what happens when many farms produce more at once. Farm jobs follow a simple rule:

Growth in farm jobs = growth in farm output − growth in output per worker

Robots raise output per worker. So farm jobs can only grow if output grows even faster. Whether it can depends on where the extra food goes.

While the country imports the crop

The local price is set by the cost of imports. Extra local output replaces imports and the price holds. Farms can expand a lot, so jobs can rise even with robots. This is today’s situation for rice, wheat and vegetable oils in much of Africa.

Once the country feeds itself

Extra output has nowhere to go, so the price falls. People do not eat much more when food gets cheaper, so output can grow only slowly. Higher productivity then pushes labor out of farming. Consumers gain, but the jobs must come from other sectors.

Farm jobs are also only part of the story. Robots affect employment through four channels:

  1. Jobs on farms, as above.
  2. Jobs in robot services: operators, mechanics, logistics.
  3. Jobs in processing and trade. More local grain means more milling, storage, transport and retail.
  4. Jobs from cheaper food. Households that spend less on food buy other things.

The largest gains are likely in channels 2 and 3. And every tonne that replaces an import saves foreign exchange.

A lesson from Brazil. When a labor-saving technology spread in soy farming, workers left agriculture and industry grew. When a technology that let farmers grow a second maize crop spread, labor stayed on farms (Bustos, Caprettini and Ponticelli, 2016). The type of technology decided where the workers went.

Step 8

Policy: plan ahead

The analysis gives one rule and one warning.

The rule: target the bottleneck, not the robot. Public money should go where a labor shortage is holding production back, and where the extra output has a market. A robot is worth supporting only for what it unblocks.

The warning: the favourable window is temporary. Today, import dependence and idle land make job-creating automation possible. As a country closes its food gap, the same robots start to release labor. A plan must cover both phases.

A plan in three horizons

Now to 2 yearsPrepare

Goal: know where the bottlenecks are before the machines arrive.

  • Map labor calendars by crop and zone. Where is land idle, or planted late, for lack of peak labor?
  • Write clear rules for drones and autonomous machines: licensing, safety, spray drift, and who owns the farm data.
  • Pilot through service providers, not equipment giveaways. In each pilot, measure how much of the robot’s work expands production.
  • Stop subsidising machines that replace available workers. They cost public money and destroy jobs.
2 to 7 yearsBuild the service market

Goal: make robot services viable where they create jobs.

  • Finance the providers: leasing, pay-per-hectare contracts and credit guarantees.
  • Group the demand through cooperatives, aggregators and block farming, so providers reach break-even.
  • Train young people as operators, mechanics and data technicians. These are the new service jobs.
  • Fix the basics: rural energy, connectivity and a reliable supply of spare parts.
  • Start with import-competing crops such as rice, wheat and oilseeds, and invest in local processing so the value-chain jobs stay in the country.
7 to 15 yearsManage the transition

Goal: be ready when farming starts to release labor.

  • Open new markets, regional and export, before the home market is full. This keeps output growing and extends the job-creating phase.
  • Build jobs off the farm: agro-processing, rural services and secondary towns that can absorb workers.
  • Protect those who lose work, especially landless and seasonal workers: safety nets, retraining and access to land.
  • Spread ownership through cooperatives and youth-owned service firms, so robot income does not concentrate in a few hands.

Five questions before funding a robot programme

  1. Which task, and is labor really short for it at that time of year?If workers are available, stop here.
  2. Where will the extra output go?It should replace imports or have a buyer. Otherwise prices fall and farmers lose.
  3. Does the robot do the task much better, or only slightly cheaper?Small savings do not make up for lost wages.
  4. Who will own and run it, and can they break even?Check the working days per year, the distance between plots and the repair chain.
  5. Who loses work, and what is planned for them?Name the group and fund the response before the programme starts.

What this analysis does not tell us yet

This is theory. It shows which conditions matter and in which direction, but it does not measure them. Two numbers need field evidence before large programmes are designed: the share of robot work that expands production in each farming system, and the days per year a provider can work. Both can be measured in pilots, which is one more reason to start with them.
