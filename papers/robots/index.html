Robots in production: a unified theory of automation, work and households

Three nested models — a fully robotic firm, robots working with people, and robots combined with capital and skills — solved for firm and household choices. One question runs through all of them: when do robots raise wages, and when do they replace workers?

Tebila Nakelse, PhD · Agricultural and labor economist · Working paper, November 2026

Start here

The paper in one minute

A robot is a new kind of input. Like a machine, it is built, bought and owned. Like a worker, it performs tasks. This paper works out what that means for wages, jobs and incomes, and then applies the answer to African farming.

  • Cheaper robots raise output, and in the long run they raise wages.
  • Workers can still lose ground. Their share of income falls when robots replace them easily, and wages can fall for years while the economy adjusts.
  • The type of automation matters more than its speed. Robots that remove a bottleneck or bring new tasks help workers. Robots that are only slightly cheaper than people hurt them.
  • On African farms, robots can create jobs when they ease the peak-season labor shortage and the extra food replaces imports.

How the paper is built

Each model adds one ingredient to the one before. The later parts ask who decides, where the economy ends up, and what it means for Africa.

Seven results the model delivers

1. Robot demand follows a user-cost rule

A firm adds robots until one more robot earns its rental cost: price × (interest + depreciation + expected price decline) + running costs. Expected cheaper robots tomorrow delay adoption today.

Part 1 of 7

Model 1 — The fully robotic firm

Start with the simplest case: a firm that produces only with robots. This isolates the economics of the machine itself, before people enter the picture.

In plain words

The question. How many robots should a firm use when robots do all the work?

The answer. It adds robots until the last one just pays for its yearly cost. So when robots get cheaper, the firm wants many more of them. And if nothing else limits production, growth depends only on how productive robots are compared with their price.

Static problem

Output is \(Y = A R^{\theta}\), where \(R\) is the robot stock, \(A\) is technology and \(0<\theta<1\) captures limited space, management or demand. The firm rents robots at the user cost \(c_R\):

\[ c_R = p_R\left(r + \delta_R - \hat{p}_R\right) + m \]

Here \(p_R\) is the robot price, \(r\) the interest rate, \(\delta_R\) depreciation, \(\hat p_R\) the expected growth rate of robot prices and \(m\) maintenance and energy per robot. Because robot prices usually fall (\(\hat p_R<0\)), expected obsolescence adds to the cost of owning one today.

Example. A robot costs 100. Interest is 4%, wear is 10% and robot prices fall by 5% a year. Running costs are 2. The yearly user cost is 100 × (0.04 + 0.10 + 0.05) + 2 = 21.

The firm maximises \(\pi = A R^{\theta} - c_R R\). The first-order condition \(\theta A R^{\theta-1} = c_R\) gives the optimum:

\[ R^{*} = \left(\frac{\theta A}{c_R}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}, \qquad Y^{*} = A^{\frac{1}{1-\theta}}\left(\frac{\theta}{c_R}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}}, \qquad \pi^{*} = (1-\theta)\,Y^{*} \]
Proposition 1. Robot demand has elasticity \(-1/(1-\theta)\) with respect to user cost. A 10% fall in robot prices raises the robot stock by more than 10%, and by much more when returns are close to constant (\(\theta \to 1\)). For example, with \(\theta=0.5\), a 10% fall in user cost raises the robot stock by about 23%.

Dynamic problem

Over time, the firm invests \(I_t\), with \(\dot R = I - \delta_R R\), and pays a convex cost \(\tfrac{\phi}{2}(\dot R/R)^2R\) whenever it changes its robot stock. The shadow value of a robot \(q\) gives Tobin’s q rule \(\dot R/R = (q - p_R)/\phi\). In steady state the stock is constant, so \(q=p_R\), and the dynamic optimum collapses to the static rule \(\theta A R^{\theta-1}=c_R\). So the static solution is the long-run target, and adjustment costs only set the speed of approach.

Constant returns: the automation growth engine

Now let \(\theta=1\), so \(Y = A_R R\). There is no interior optimum: if \(A_R > c_R\), each robot earns a pure profit and the firm expands without limit. In general equilibrium, households own the robots and save according to the Euler equation with risk aversion \(\eta\) and time preference \(\rho\):

\[ g = \frac{\dot C}{C} = \frac{1}{\eta}\left(\frac{A_R}{p_R} - \delta_R - \rho\right) \]
Proposition 2. A fully automated economy is an AK economy: output is proportional to the robot stock, so growth can continue without more workers. Its growth rate depends on robot productivity per unit of robot price, not on population or labor. This is the theoretical benchmark against which every mixed system below is measured.

Part 2 of 7

Model 2 — Robots working with people

People now enter. There are two standard ways to model this, and they lead to different conclusions, so we solve both.

In plain words

The question. What happens to workers when robots work beside them?

The answer. It depends on what a robot is. If a robot is like a better tool that makes each worker more productive (2A), cheaper robots always raise wages. If a robot takes over whole tasks (2B), wages can fall for a time, and workers’ share of income falls for good unless new tasks appear.

2A. Robots as a factor (CES)

\[ Y = A\left[\omega L^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} + (1-\omega)(\gamma R)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\right]^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}} \]

\(\sigma\) is the elasticity of substitution between labor and robots, \(\omega\) the labor weight and \(\gamma\) robot efficiency. Cost minimisation at wage \(w\) and user cost \(c_R\) gives the optimal robot-labor ratio and the labor share of costs:

\[ \frac{R}{L} = \gamma^{\sigma-1}\left(\frac{1-\omega}{\omega}\right)^{\sigma}\left(\frac{w}{c_R}\right)^{\sigma}, \qquad s_L = \frac{\omega^{\sigma}w^{1-\sigma}}{\omega^{\sigma}w^{1-\sigma} + (1-\omega)^{\sigma}(c_R/\gamma)^{1-\sigma}} \]

The firm’s choice is governed by one relative price: the wage compared with the robot’s user cost, \(w/c_R\). When robots get cheaper, firms shift toward them. Wages rise, and the labor share falls if and only if robots replace workers easily (\(\sigma>1\)).

2B. Robots as task performers (task-based)

Think of production as a line of tasks, as in the picture at the top of the page. Robots can do the tasks on the left, up to a frontier \(I\). People do the rest, up to the newest task \(N\). Automation moves \(I\) to the right. New tasks move \(N\) to the right.

Production combines a continuum of tasks \(i\in[N-1,N]\): \(\ln Y = \int_{N-1}^{N}\ln y(i)\,di\). A task can use labor with productivity \(\gamma_L(i)\) or, if it is technically automatable (\(i\le I\)), robots with productivity \(\gamma_R(i)\). People have a comparative advantage in higher-indexed tasks: \(\gamma_L(i)/\gamma_R(i)\) rises with \(i\).

The firm automates a task when robots are cheaper per unit of task output. Let \(r_R\) be the market rental rate of a robot. In the short run the robot stock is given and \(r_R\) clears the robot market. In the long run new robots are built until \(r_R\) falls to the user cost \(c_R\). The profitable threshold \(\tilde I\) and the equilibrium frontier \(I^*\) are:

\[ \frac{w}{\gamma_L(\tilde I)} = \frac{r_R}{\gamma_R(\tilde I)}, \qquad I^{*} = \min\{I,\ \tilde I\}, \qquad s_L = N - I^{*} \]

Each task receives an equal share of spending, so labor’s share is simply the share of tasks people still perform. Holding the stocks of robots and labor fixed, the wage effect of automating more tasks splits into two forces:

\[ \frac{d\ln w}{dI} = \underbrace{\ln\!\left(\frac{w/\gamma_L(I)}{r_R/\gamma_R(I)}\right)}_{\text{productivity effect}\ \ge 0} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{N-I}}_{\text{displacement effect}} \]
  • Productivity effect (raises wages). The firm saves money on the automated task. It produces more, so it needs more of every other task, including those people do.
  • Displacement effect (lowers wages). The same workers now compete for fewer tasks.

Example. Suppose people still do 60% of tasks. The displacement effect is 1/0.6 ≈ 1.67. The productivity effect is larger only if a person costs more than five times as much as a robot on that task (\(e^{1.67}\approx 5.3\)). Any smaller saving lowers wages in the short run.

Proposition 3. While the robot stock is fixed, automation raises wages only if its cost saving exceeds \(1/(N-I)\). “So-so” automation — robots barely cheaper than people — lowers wages and the labor share together. In the long run, as robots accumulate until \(r_R=c_R\), the wage recovers: \(d\ln w/dI = \ln\!\big(\tfrac{w/\gamma_L}{c_R/\gamma_R}\big)/(N-I)\ge 0\). The labor share, however, stays lower at \(N-I\). Creating new tasks (raising \(N\)) has the opposite reinstatement effect: it lifts both wages and the labor share.

This is the central contrast. In 2A, more robots always raise wages. In 2B, automation can lower wages during the transition, which may last years, and it lowers labor’s share for good unless new tasks appear. The calculator below lets you test the short-run threshold directly.

Part 3 of 7

Model 3 — Robots, capital and people together

Real production systems mix three inputs: traditional capital \(K\) (buildings, tractors, land improvements), robots \(R\) and labor \(L\). Robots compete with routine labor inside an “automation bundle” \(X\), and that bundle combines with capital.

In plain words

The question. What changes when buildings, machines and skills are added?

The answer. Two things move in opposite directions. Cheaper robots raise wages in the long run. Yet workers’ share of income falls when robots replace them easily (\(\sigma>1\)). And skilled workers gain more than routine workers when robots replace routine work more easily than skills do.

\[ Y = A K^{\alpha} X^{1-\alpha}, \qquad X = \left[\omega L^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} + (1-\omega)(\gamma R)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\right]^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}} \]

Solving the firm’s problem

With capital rented at \(c_K=r+\delta_K\), the capital condition \(\alpha Y/K = c_K\) can be substituted out. Output becomes linear in the bundle, \(Y = \Psi X\), with

\[ \Psi = A^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{c_K}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}} \]

After capital is paid, a unit of the bundle is worth \(\tilde\Psi = (1-\alpha)\Psi\). The robot condition \(\tilde\Psi\,\partial X/\partial R = c_R\) then has a closed-form solution for \(s\), the robots’ share of the bundle:

\[ s = (1-\omega)^{\sigma}\left(\frac{\gamma\tilde\Psi}{c_R}\right)^{\sigma-1}, \qquad \frac{X}{L} = \left(\frac{\omega}{1-s}\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}} \]

Factor shares and the wage follow directly:

\[ \text{capital: }\alpha, \qquad \text{robots: }(1-\alpha)s, \qquad \text{labor: }(1-\alpha)(1-s), \qquad w = \tilde\Psi\,\omega^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}}(1-s)^{-\frac{1}{\sigma-1}} \]
Proposition 4. In the long run, a fall in robot cost always raises the wage, for any \(\sigma\). But it lowers the labor share when \(\sigma>1\) and raises it when \(\sigma<1\). Workers can be better off in absolute terms while losing ground relative to capital and robot owners.

Adding skills

Split labor into skilled \(H\) and routine \(L\). Skilled workers combine with the automation bundle with elasticity \(\varepsilon\): the higher it is, the more easily skills and the bundle replace each other.

\[ Y = A K^{\alpha}\left[\mu H^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}} + (1-\mu)X^{\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon}}\right]^{\frac{(1-\alpha)\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-1}}, \qquad \frac{w_H}{w_L} = \frac{\mu}{(1-\mu)\,\omega}\,H^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\,X^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\sigma}}\,L^{\frac{1}{\sigma}} \]
Proposition 5. More robots raise \(X\). The skill premium therefore rises with automation if and only if \(\sigma > \varepsilon\): robots substitute for routine work more easily than skilled work substitutes for the routine bundle. This is the robot analogue of capital–skill complementarity.

Part 4 of 7

Firm-level decision: who adopts robots?

In plain words

The question. Which firms buy robots first?

The answer. Robots come with a fixed cost: buying, installing and learning to use them. Only firms that are productive enough earn that cost back. So large firms adopt first. Smaller ones follow when leasing or shared services lower the entry cost.

Adoption is lumpy. Firms differ in productivity \(\varphi\) and face decreasing returns \(\nu<1\) on a constant-returns input bundle with unit cost \(c_j\). Technology \(j=0\) uses labor and capital; \(j=1\) adds robots and lowers unit cost to \(c_1<c_0\), at an annualised fixed cost \(F\). Maximised profit is

\[ \pi_j(\varphi) = (1-\nu)\,\nu^{\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}}\,\varphi^{\frac{1}{1-\nu}}\,c_j^{-\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}} \]

The firm adopts if \(\pi_1(\varphi)-\pi_0(\varphi)\ge F\). Because the gain grows with \(\varphi\), there is a single cut-off:

\[ \varphi^{*} = \left[\frac{F}{(1-\nu)\,\nu^{\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}}\left(c_1^{-\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}} - c_0^{-\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}}\right)}\right]^{1-\nu} \]
Proposition 6. Only firms above \(\varphi^*\) automate, so adoption starts with the largest and most productive firms. Adopters expand output and can hire more workers, while non-adopting competitors lose market share. Lower fixed costs — through leasing, shared services or credit — reach smaller firms first by lowering \(\varphi^*\).

This matches firm-level evidence from Europe: robot adopters tend to grow employment, while the job losses appear among their competitors.

Part 5 of 7

Household-level decisions

In plain words

The question. How do households decide, and who receives the income robots earn?

The answer. Households save, and robots are one of the assets they can own. Those who own robots receive the robot income. Those who only work receive wages. On farms, households that are short of labor hire robot services first.

Households as savers and robot owners

A representative household chooses consumption \(c\), hours \(h\) and investment in capital and robots to maximise \(\int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t}u(c,1-h)\,dt\), subject to

\[ c + \dot K + p_R\dot R = w h + (r_K - \delta_K)K + (r_R - p_R\delta_R)R \]

Three conditions characterise the solution: the labor supply rule \(u_\ell/u_c = w\); the Euler equation \(\dot c/c = (r-\rho)/\eta\); and no-arbitrage between the two assets, \(r_K-\delta_K = r_R/p_R - \delta_R\). In steady state \(r=\rho\), which pins down the user costs used by firms in Models 1–3.

Households save in two assets, ordinary capital and robots, and both must earn the same return. This rule sets the robot user cost that firms face in Models 1 to 3.

Workers and owners

Ownership matters for welfare. Suppose a share of households own only their labor and consume \(c^W = wh\), while owners hold \(K\) and \(R\). Then the income gap between owners and workers equals the ratio of their factor shares.

\[ \frac{\text{owner income}}{\text{worker income}} = \frac{\alpha + (1-\alpha)s}{(1-\alpha)(1-s)} \]

With \(\sigma>1\), cheaper robots raise \(s\) and widen this gap, even though worker income rises. Broad ownership of robots — cooperatives, pension funds, shared service firms — keeps the gains from concentrating.

The agricultural household with robot services

For farms, robots usually arrive as services: drone spraying, robotic weeding, hired automated harvesting.

A farm household with land \(T\) maximises \(u(c,\ell)\) subject to

\[ c = pF(L_f, S;\,T) - w(L_f - L_o) - qS + w L_m, \qquad E = \ell + L_o + L_m \]

where \(L_f\) is total farm labor, \(L_o\) own labor on the farm, \(L_m\) off-farm work, \(S\) robot services at price \(q\) and \(E\) the time endowment. With complete labor markets the model is separable: the farm sets \(pF_L=w\) and \(pF_S=q\), independent of preferences. When the labor market is thin, the household uses a shadow wage \(w^{*}=u_\ell/u_c\) instead, so

\[ \frac{S}{L_f} \ \text{rises with}\ \frac{w^{*}}{q} \]

When a household cannot easily hire workers, an hour of its own labor is worth more than the market wage. This is its shadow wage \(w^{*}\). Labor-scarce households — those with migrant members, older heads or youth in school — have a higher shadow wage and adopt robot services first. Labor-rich households adopt later.

Part 6 of 7

General equilibrium and the automation growth regime

In plain words

The question. Where does all this lead in the long run?

The answer. Usually to a stable mix of robots and people. But if robots become cheap enough and replace workers easily, there is no stable mix: robots keep accumulating. The economy then grows without needing more workers, and incomes depend mostly on who owns the robots.

Closing Model 3 with households, the steady state sets \(r=\rho\), so \(c_K=\rho+\delta_K\) and \(c_R=p_R(\rho+\delta_R)\). The closed-form share \(s\) has a natural upper limit. When robots and labor are substitutes (\(\sigma>1\)) and robots become cheap enough that

\[ \frac{\gamma\tilde\Psi}{c_R} \ \ge\ (1-\omega)^{-\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}} \]

the marginal robot never stops paying for itself. No steady state exists. The economy enters an automation growth regime: robots accumulate without limit, growth becomes endogenous as in Model 1’s AK benchmark, the labor share tends to zero and wages still grow, at rate \(g/\sigma\).

Proposition 7. Mixed robot–human systems converge to a steady state unless robot cost falls below a critical threshold set by \(\sigma\), \(\omega\) and \(\gamma\). Beyond it, the economy shifts from Model 3 to Model 1: growth without labor scarcity, and distribution governed almost entirely by who owns the robots.

Part 7 of 7

Robots in low-income smallholder agriculture: Africa first

Most theory of automation was built for rich economies where labor is scarce and expensive. Low-income agriculture looks very different: farms are small, family labor does most of the work, and wages are low. So the question is not whether robots raise productivity, but when they do so without destroying the jobs most rural people depend on. The same logic applies to South Asia and the wider Global South; Africa is the focus here.

In plain words

The question. Do farm robots create or destroy jobs where labor is cheap?

The answer. Both are possible. They create jobs when they ease the peak-season labor shortage, which lets farmers cultivate more land, and when the extra food replaces imports. They destroy jobs when they only replace cheap workers on land that would be farmed anyway.

African farm output has grown mainly by adding land and labor, not by producing more per worker or per hectare. Recent household panel data even find stagnant or falling productivity in several countries. Meanwhile, urban demand is set to double. The gap is filled by imports, which drain foreign exchange and expose countries to world price spikes, currency shocks and supply disruptions. This is why food sovereignty — the ability to feed cities largely from domestic farms — has become a political priority.

Labor is the puzzle at the centre. Africa has abundant rural labor across the year, yet farms face sharp seasonal shortages. Because neighbours plant, weed and harvest at the same time, labor demand peaks together, and many farms cannot find workers when it matters. As rural youth move to towns, these peaks are getting tighter. A useful model must therefore capture both facts at once: underemployment on average, and a binding labor bottleneck at the peak.

Model A — The two-season smallholder

A household owns \(T\) hectares. Each cultivated hectare yields \(\bar y\) at price \(p\) and needs two kinds of work:

  • Peak task (land preparation, planting, harvest): \(a\) hours per hectare inside a short window. Household peak labor is \(E_p\); hired peak labor costs \(w_p\).
  • Other tasks (weeding, post-harvest, marketing): \(b\) hours per hectare at the slack-season wage \(w_o\), with labor plentiful.

A robot service does the peak task on one hectare at price \(q\).

Cultivated area is limited by peak capacity:

\[ T_c = \min\left\{T,\ \frac{E_p + H}{a} + S\right\} \]

where \(H\) is hired peak labor and \(S\) the hectares served by robots. The household compares two costs of finishing the peak task on a hectare: \(a\,w_p^{*}\) with people, or \(q\) with robots. The key variable is the shadow peak wage \(w_p^{*}\):

\[ w_p^{*} = \begin{cases} w_p & \text{if peak labor can be hired freely} \\[4pt] \dfrac{p\bar y - b\,w_o}{a} & \text{if the peak constraint binds and land is idle} \end{cases} \qquad \text{Adopt robots} \iff q < a\,w_p^{*} \]

The key idea is the shadow peak wage: what an hour of peak labor is really worth to the household. If workers can be hired freely, it is simply the market wage. If they cannot, and land stays idle, it equals the full net value of the hectare that would otherwise go uncultivated. That can be many times the market wage. The household adopts the robot service when its price is below the value of the peak labor it replaces.

Example (illustrative numbers). A hectare yields a crop worth 400. Other tasks cost 300 hours × 0.5 = 150. The peak task needs 150 hours. If no workers can be found at the peak, each peak hour is worth (400 − 150) / 150 ≈ 1.7, more than three times the market wage of 0.5. So a robot service pays at any price below 250 per hectare, although hired workers would cost only 75 if they were available.

The job-creation condition

Let \(\psi\in[0,1]\) be the share of robot work that expands production — new area, a second season, or yield saved by timely work — rather than replacing people on existing work. Let \(e\) be the operator, repair and logistics hours each robot-hectare creates. One more robot-served hectare then changes labor demand through three channels:

  • Expansion (adds work): \(\psi\,b\) hours of other tasks on land that now produces more.
  • Displacement (removes work): \((1-\psi)\,a\) hours of peak work that people no longer do.
  • New service tasks (adds work): \(e\) hours to operate, move and repair the robot.
\[ \frac{dL}{dS} = \underbrace{\psi\, b}_{\text{expansion}} \;-\; \underbrace{(1-\psi)\,a}_{\text{displacement}} \;+\; \underbrace{e}_{\text{new service tasks}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dL}{dS} > 0 \iff \psi > \frac{a-e}{a+b} \]

Example. Jobs rise when \(\psi\) is above the threshold \(\psi^{*}=(a-e)/(a+b)\). With 150 peak hours, 300 other hours and 10 service hours per hectare, \(\psi^{*}=140/450\approx 0.31\). So robots create jobs if at least about a third of their work expands production. The calculator below lets you change these numbers.

Proposition 8. On smallholder farms, robots create net jobs when they relieve a binding bottleneck (high \(\psi\)), when the rest of the crop cycle is labor-intensive (high \(b\)), and when they bring new service tasks (high \(e\)). They destroy jobs when they replace cheap labor on land that would be farmed anyway (\(\psi\to 0\)).

Model B — Can a robot service survive in a smallholder market?

Few smallholders can buy a robot, so robots arrive through service providers. A provider must serve enough hectares to cover its fixed cost, but the season is short and plots are scattered, so that number is hard to reach.

A provider with fixed cost \(F\) per season, variable cost \(m\) per hectare and daily capacity \(k\) can work only during a window of \(W\) days. It breaks even if

\[ U^{*} = \frac{F}{q-m} \ \le\ \min\{kW,\ D(q)\} \]

where \(D(q)\) is local demand.

Three features of African farming push against viability: short, synchronised windows (small \(kW\)), scattered plots that raise travel cost \(m\), and thin demand that limits \(D(q)\). The remedies follow from this logic: multi-task robots that work in several seasons, providers that move across agro-ecological zones as harvests ripen, clustering of farms through cooperatives or aggregators, and finance that spreads \(F\).

Model C — Economy-wide effects in a food-importing country

In plain words

While a country still imports a food, extra local output replaces imports and prices hold. Farms can then produce much more, and employ more people, even with robots. Once imports are gone, extra output pushes prices down, and farming sheds labor.

Now step back to the whole economy.

Agricultural employment equals output divided by labor productivity, \(L_A = Q_A/\pi_A\), so its growth rate is

\[ \hat L_A = \hat Q_A - \hat\pi_A \]

Robots raise output per worker, \(\pi_A\). Whether farm jobs rise or fall depends on how fast output \(Q_A\) can grow, and that depends on the trade regime:

  • Import-parity regime (today’s Africa for rice, wheat, oils). The domestic price is set by imports, so extra domestic output replaces imports instead of pushing prices down. Output can grow fast: \(\hat Q_A\) is driven by supply response, not by limited demand. Farm jobs rise if \(\hat Q_A > \hat\pi_A\).
  • Self-sufficiency (non-tradable) regime. Once imports are gone, extra output must lower prices. With inelastic food demand (\(\varepsilon_D\) well below one), \(\hat Q_A\) is small, so higher productivity pushes labor out of farming. Urban consumers gain, but jobs must then come from other sectors.

Adding up, farm robots change total employment through four channels: jobs on farms, jobs in robot services, jobs in processing and trade, and jobs created when households spend what they save on cheaper food. The import bill also falls, which saves foreign exchange.

In symbols, the economy-wide job effect of agricultural robots is

\[ \Delta J = \underbrace{L_A(\hat Q_A - \hat\pi_A)}_{\text{farm jobs}} + \underbrace{e\,S}_{\text{robot services}} + \underbrace{\mu\,\Delta Q_A}_{\text{processing and trade}} + \underbrace{\zeta\,(-\Delta p_F)\,C_F}_{\text{spending from cheaper food}} \]

Here \(\mu\) is jobs per unit of extra output along the value chain and \(\zeta\) the jobs created when households spend food savings elsewhere. The import bill falls by \(p_M\,\Delta Q_A\).

Proposition 9. In a food-importing economy, labor-saving robots in agriculture can raise total farm employment, because their output replaces imports rather than collapsing prices. The job gains are largest in the processing and service chain. Once self-sufficiency is reached, further robots release labor, and the outcome turns on whether manufacturing and services can absorb it. Brazil’s experience shows that labor-saving farm technology pushed workers into industry, while land-augmenting technology kept them on farms.

When robots help, and when they don’t

Robots help when…Robots don’t help when…
A peak-season bottleneck leaves land idle or work lateLabor is plentiful and cheap at the peak
The task is one people do badly: precision spraying, scouting, hazardous or drudging workRobots only match people on cost (“so-so” automation)
The country imports the crop, so output replaces importsThe market is saturated and food demand is inelastic
Services are shared, farms are clustered and windows can be stretchedPlots are scattered, windows short and repair or energy unreliable
The rest of the value chain is labor-intensive (processing, horticulture)Displaced workers are landless and have nowhere else to go

South Asia contrast. With farms often around one hectare and little idle land, the area-expansion channel is weak. There, \(\psi\) comes mainly from cropping intensity: fast turnaround between rice and wheat allows a second crop. Custom-hire markets for combine harvesters show the service model can work at scale.

Bottom line. In Africa’s current situation — import dependence, seasonal bottlenecks and, in many places, land still available — targeted robots in bottleneck tasks are likely to create jobs on balance, especially in services and processing. Blanket automation of tasks people already do cheaply would destroy jobs. Policy should target the bottleneck, not the robot.
